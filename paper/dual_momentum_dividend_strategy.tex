\begin{abstract}
This paper presents a systematic equity selection strategy that combines Gary Antonacci's dual momentum framework with a novel inverse dividend yield weighting scheme. We apply the strategy to a universe of 132 dividend-paying U.S. large-cap stocks, employing absolute momentum as a timing mechanism and relative momentum for cross-sectional stock selection. The key innovation lies in weighting selected securities inversely proportional to their trailing dividend yield, using yield compression as a momentum confirmation signal. Our backtest spanning March 2020 to November 2025 with 2$\times$ leverage demonstrates a total return of 1,942.77\%, an annualized return of 64.56\%, and a Sharpe ratio of 1.31. We provide comprehensive mathematical formalization of all components, detailed risk analysis, and discuss the strategy's behavior across market regimes. While results are promising, we emphasize important caveats regarding the favorable backtest period and the need for out-of-sample validation.
\end{abstract}

\textbf{Keywords:} Momentum investing, dividend yield, dual momentum, quantitative strategy, portfolio construction, risk-adjusted returns

\section{Introduction}
%==============================================================================

Momentum strategies have been a cornerstone of quantitative finance since the seminal work of \citet{jegadeesh1993returns}, who documented that stocks with strong past returns tend to continue outperforming over intermediate horizons. This anomaly persists across asset classes, geographies, and time periods, presenting both an empirical puzzle and a profitable trading opportunity.

Parallel to momentum research, dividend-paying stocks have attracted considerable academic and practitioner attention. The dividend yield has been shown to predict both individual stock returns and aggregate market returns, serving as both a valuation metric and a quality signal \citep{fama1988dividend}. Companies with consistent dividend payment histories typically exhibit superior earnings stability, lower leverage, and more disciplined capital allocation.

This paper introduces a quantitative strategy that synthesizes these two research streams through a novel application: using inverse dividend yield as a momentum confirmation signal within a dividend-paying stock universe. The core insight is that within a universe of dividend-paying stocks, a lower dividend yield indicates recent price appreciation---the price has risen faster than dividends, compressing the yield. This yield compression thus serves as an independent confirmation of momentum strength.

\subsection{Contribution}

Our primary contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Inverse Yield Weighting}: We propose using the reciprocal of dividend yield as portfolio weights, providing momentum confirmation within a quality-screened universe.

    \item \textbf{Quality-Filtered Momentum}: We implement a signal-to-noise filter requiring momentum to exceed a threshold proportion of volatility, reducing false signals.

    \item \textbf{Comprehensive Backtest}: We provide detailed backtest results including cost attribution, regime analysis, and benchmark comparison with explicit leverage and borrowing cost modeling.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Literature Review}
\label{sec:literature}
%==============================================================================

\subsection{Momentum Anomaly}

The momentum effect, first systematically documented by \citet{jegadeesh1993returns}, demonstrates that stocks with high returns over the past 3-12 months continue to outperform over subsequent months. This finding has been replicated extensively and remains one of the most robust anomalies in finance.

\citet{carhart1997persistence} formalized momentum as a risk factor, extending the Fama-French three-factor model. The momentum factor (UMD, or ``Up Minus Down'') captures the return difference between past winners and losers and has demonstrated significant positive returns across decades.

\subsection{Dual Momentum}

\citet{antonacci2014dual} introduced the dual momentum concept, combining:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Relative (Cross-Sectional) Momentum}: Ranking assets by past returns and selecting top performers.
    \item \textbf{Absolute (Time-Series) Momentum}: Investing only when an asset's return exceeds a benchmark (typically zero or a risk-free rate).
\end{enumerate}

The combination of these filters has been shown to improve risk-adjusted returns by avoiding extended drawdowns during bear markets while capturing upside during trends.

\subsection{Dividend Yield and Stock Returns}

The dividend yield has a rich history as a return predictor. \citet{fama1988dividend} demonstrated its ability to forecast aggregate market returns, while \citet{campbell1988stock} showed yield predicts returns through both expected return and dividend growth channels.

At the individual stock level, high dividend yield stocks have been associated with the value premium \citep{fama1992cross}. However, the relationship is nuanced: very high yields may signal financial distress (``yield traps''), while moderate yields often indicate quality and shareholder-friendly management.

\subsection{Quality Investing}

The quality factor, formalized by \citet{asness2019quality}, encompasses characteristics including profitability, earnings stability, and low leverage. Dividend-paying stocks naturally screen for many quality characteristics, as consistent dividend payment requires stable cash flows and disciplined capital management.

Our strategy leverages this by restricting the investment universe to established dividend payers, then using momentum and yield dynamics for selection and weighting.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}
%==============================================================================

\subsection{Mathematical Framework}

Let $\mathcal{U}$ denote our investment universe of $N$ dividend-paying stocks. For stock $i$ at time $t$, we define:

\begin{itemize}
    \item $P_{i,t}$: Adjusted closing price
    \item $D_{i,t}$: Dividend per share (trailing 12 months)
    \item $r_{i,t}^{(k)}$: $k$-day return ending at $t$
    \item $\sigma_{i,t}$: Annualized volatility
    \item $Y_{i,t}$: Trailing 12-month dividend yield
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Momentum Calculation}

The 12-month momentum for stock $i$ at time $t$ is defined as:

\begin{equation}
\text{MOM}_{i,t} = \frac{P_{i,t}}{P_{i,t-252}} - 1
\label{eq:momentum}
\end{equation}

where 252 represents the number of trading days in one year. This measure captures the total price return over the lookback period.

\subsubsection{Volatility Estimation}

We estimate annualized volatility using a 63-day (quarterly) rolling window:

\begin{equation}
\sigma_{i,t} = \sqrt{252} \cdot \sqrt{\frac{1}{62} \sum_{j=1}^{63} \left( r_{i,t-j+1} - \bar{r}_{i,t} \right)^2}
\label{eq:volatility}
\end{equation}

where $r_{i,t}$ denotes daily returns and $\bar{r}_{i,t}$ is the sample mean over the estimation window.

\subsubsection{Quality Filter}

To distinguish genuine momentum from noise, we require that momentum exceeds a threshold proportion of volatility:

\begin{definition}[Quality-Filtered Momentum]
Stock $i$ passes the quality filter at time $t$ if and only if:
\begin{equation}
\text{MOM}_{i,t} > \kappa \cdot \sigma_{i,t}
\label{eq:quality_filter}
\end{equation}
where $\kappa = 0.75$ is the quality threshold multiplier.
\end{definition}

This filter eliminates stocks where observed momentum may be attributable to random fluctuation rather than genuine trend. The threshold of 0.75 requires momentum to be approximately 0.75 standard deviations above zero on an annualized basis.

\subsubsection{Dividend Yield}

The trailing 12-month dividend yield is:

\begin{equation}
Y_{i,t} = \frac{\sum_{j=0}^{251} D_{i,t-j}}{P_{i,t}}
\label{eq:dividend_yield}
\end{equation}

We impose a floor of 0.5\% to prevent extreme weights:

\begin{equation}
\tilde{Y}_{i,t} = \max(Y_{i,t}, 0.005)
\label{eq:yield_floor}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Selection Algorithm}

The dual momentum selection process proceeds as follows:

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Dual Momentum Stock Selection}
\label{alg:selection}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Universe $\mathcal{U}$, date $t$, parameters $\kappa$, $K$
\Ensure Selected stocks $\mathcal{S}_t$ with weights $\{w_{i,t}\}$

\State \textbf{Step 1: Absolute Momentum Filter}
\State $\mathcal{A}_t \gets \{i \in \mathcal{U} : \text{MOM}_{i,t} > 0\}$

\State \textbf{Step 2: Quality Filter}
\State $\mathcal{Q}_t \gets \{i \in \mathcal{A}_t : \text{MOM}_{i,t} > \kappa \cdot \sigma_{i,t}\}$

\State \textbf{Step 3: Relative Momentum Ranking}
\State Rank stocks in $\mathcal{Q}_t$ by $\text{MOM}_{i,t}$ in descending order

\State \textbf{Step 4: Top-$K$ Selection}
\State $\mathcal{S}_t \gets$ top $K$ stocks from ranked list

\State \textbf{Step 5: Inverse Yield Weighting}
\For{$i \in \mathcal{S}_t$}
    \State $\omega_i \gets 1 / \tilde{Y}_{i,t}$
\EndFor
\State $w_{i,t} \gets \omega_i / \sum_{j \in \mathcal{S}_t} \omega_j$ for all $i \in \mathcal{S}_t$

\State \Return $\mathcal{S}_t$, $\{w_{i,t}\}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Inverse Yield Weighting: Economic Rationale}

The inverse yield weighting scheme is motivated by the following proposition:

\begin{proposition}[Yield Compression as Momentum Confirmation]
Within a universe of dividend-paying stocks, lower dividend yield indicates stronger recent price momentum, holding dividend policy constant.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Consider two stocks $i$ and $j$ with identical annual dividends $D$ but different prices $P_i > P_j$. Their yields are:
\[
Y_i = \frac{D}{P_i} < \frac{D}{P_j} = Y_j
\]
The higher price $P_i$ implies greater price appreciation, hence stronger momentum. Thus, lower yield serves as a confirmation of momentum within the dividend-paying universe.
\end{proof}

The inverse yield weight for stock $i$ is:

\begin{equation}
w_{i,t} = \lambda \cdot \frac{1/\tilde{Y}_{i,t}}{\sum_{j \in \mathcal{S}_t} 1/\tilde{Y}_{j,t}}
\label{eq:inverse_yield_weight}
\end{equation}

where $\lambda$ represents the leverage factor (in our case, $\lambda = 2.0$).

\subsection{Leverage Implementation}

We implement leverage by borrowing at the risk-free rate to achieve $\lambda = 2.0$ times exposure. The margin debt at time $t$ is:

\begin{equation}
M_t = (\lambda - 1) \cdot E_t
\label{eq:margin_debt}
\end{equation}

where $E_t$ is the portfolio equity. Daily borrowing cost is:

\begin{equation}
C_t^{\text{borrow}} = M_t \cdot \frac{r_f^{(t)}}{252}
\label{eq:borrowing_cost}
\end{equation}

where $r_f^{(t)}$ is the annualized risk-free rate (proxied by the 10-Year Treasury yield).

\subsection{Transaction Costs}

We model transaction costs as proportional slippage:

\begin{equation}
C_t^{\text{slip}} = \delta \cdot |V_t^{\text{trade}}|
\label{eq:slippage}
\end{equation}

where $\delta$ represents total execution costs (bid-ask spread plus market impact) and $V_t^{\text{trade}}$ is the dollar volume traded at rebalance.

\subsubsection{Slippage Rate Justification}

Our baseline slippage assumption of $\delta = 0.0002$ (2 basis points) is informed by empirical research on execution costs for large-cap equities. According to NASDAQ Economic Research \citep{mackintosh2021spreads}, bid-ask spreads for S\&P 500 constituents exhibit a convex relationship with portfolio breadth that plateaus at approximately 4.5 basis points:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Top 10 stocks} (AAPL, MSFT, etc.): $\sim$2.5 bps average spread
    \item \textbf{Top 100 stocks}: $\sim$3.7 bps average spread
    \item \textbf{Full S\&P 500}: $\sim$4.5 bps average spread (plateau)
    \item \textbf{Small-cap stocks}: 36--50+ bps (significantly wider)
\end{itemize}

Our universe of 132 large-cap dividend stocks closely mirrors the top-100 S\&P 500 constituents in terms of liquidity characteristics. Given that:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item The half-spread (one-way cost) for our universe approximates 1.5--2.0 bps
    \item We trade liquid securities with ADV $>$ \$10 million
    \item Position sizes are small relative to daily volume
    \item We employ patient execution (not requiring immediate fills)
\end{enumerate}

The 2 bps assumption represents a reasonable baseline for normal market conditions. However, we conduct sensitivity analysis across a range of 2--6 bps to account for:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Volatility-dependent spread widening (spreads spiked to $\sim$20 bps during March 2020)
    \item Market impact for larger portfolios
    \item Execution timing constraints
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Portfolio Equity Evolution}

Let $\Pi_t$ denote the portfolio value at time $t$. Between rebalances, equity evolves as:

\begin{equation}
E_t = \sum_{i \in \mathcal{S}} n_{i,t} \cdot P_{i,t} - M_t + \text{Cash}_t
\label{eq:equity}
\end{equation}

where $n_{i,t}$ is the number of shares held in stock $i$.

\subsection{Risk Metrics}

We evaluate strategy performance using standard risk-adjusted metrics:

\textbf{Sharpe Ratio:}
\begin{equation}
\text{SR} = \frac{\E[r_p - r_f]}{\sigma_p} \approx \frac{\bar{r}_p - \bar{r}_f}{s_p} \cdot \sqrt{252}
\label{eq:sharpe}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Sortino Ratio:}
\begin{equation}
\text{Sortino} = \frac{\E[r_p - r_f]}{\sigma_{\text{down}}}
\label{eq:sortino}
\end{equation}

where $\sigma_{\text{down}} = \sqrt{\E[\min(r_p - r_f, 0)^2]}$ is the downside deviation.

\textbf{Maximum Drawdown:}
\begin{equation}
\text{MDD} = \max_{t} \left( \frac{\max_{s \leq t} E_s - E_t}{\max_{s \leq t} E_s} \right)
\label{eq:max_drawdown}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Calmar Ratio:}
\begin{equation}
\text{Calmar} = \frac{\text{Annualized Return}}{|\text{MDD}|}
\label{eq:calmar}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Value at Risk (95\%):}
\begin{equation}
\text{VaR}_{0.95} = -\text{Quantile}_{0.05}(r_p)
\label{eq:var}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Conditional Value at Risk (Expected Shortfall):}
\begin{equation}
\text{CVaR}_{0.95} = -\E[r_p | r_p \leq -\text{VaR}_{0.95}]
\label{eq:cvar}
\end{equation}

\end{enumerate}

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
%==============================================================================

\subsection{Strategy Strengths}

The dual momentum strategy with inverse yield weighting demonstrates several notable strengths:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Risk-Adjusted Performance}: A Sharpe ratio of 1.31 and Sortino ratio of 1.96 indicate strong risk-adjusted returns, with particularly favorable downside risk characteristics.

    \item \textbf{Alpha Generation}: The annualized alpha of 31.79\% represents substantial outperformance beyond market exposure, suggesting genuine stock selection skill embedded in the systematic process.

    \item \textbf{Favorable Capture Asymmetry}: The upside capture ratio of 1.15$\times$ indicates the strategy participates more in market rallies than declines, a desirable characteristic for long-only strategies.

    \item \textbf{Transparency}: All components are mathematically specified and can be implemented systematically without discretionary judgment.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Limitations and Caveats}

Several important limitations must be acknowledged:

\subsubsection{Favorable Backtest Period}

The backtest commences March 19, 2020, near the COVID-19 market bottom. The subsequent period (2020-2025) represents one of the strongest momentum environments in recent history, characterized by:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Extended bull market with limited corrections
    \item Strong sector momentum in technology and healthcare
    \item Low interest rates supporting equity valuations
\end{itemize}

These conditions are favorable for momentum strategies and may not persist.

\subsubsection{Limited Adverse Regime Testing}

The backtest does not include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Extended bear markets (e.g., 2008-2009 financial crisis)
    \item Prolonged sideways/range-bound markets
    \item Momentum crash episodes (e.g., 2009 momentum reversal)
\end{itemize}

Momentum strategies historically experience significant drawdowns during sharp market reversals, which are underrepresented in our sample.

\subsubsection{High Turnover}

The strategy's turnover ratio of 4,904.5$\times$ initial capital over 5.69 years (approximately 862$\times$ annually) is substantial. While we model 2 basis points slippage:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Actual market impact may be higher, particularly for larger portfolios
    \item Bid-ask spreads vary with market conditions
    \item Implementation costs may scale nonlinearly with portfolio size
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Leverage Risk}

The 2$\times$ leverage amplifies both returns and risks:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Maximum drawdown of $-35.70\%$ would be approximately $-17.85\%$ at 1$\times$ leverage
    \item Margin calls are not explicitly modeled
    \item Borrowing costs vary with interest rate environment
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Survivorship Bias}

The 132-stock universe represents currently active dividend payers. Stocks that were dividend payers in 2020 but subsequently cut dividends or delisted may be underrepresented, potentially inflating performance estimates.

\subsection{Comparison to Literature}

The strategy's Sharpe ratio of 1.31 compares favorably to reported momentum strategy performance in the literature:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \citet{jegadeesh1993returns}: Reported momentum Sharpe ratios of approximately 0.4-0.6
    \item \citet{antonacci2014dual}: Reported dual momentum Sharpe ratios of 0.8-1.1
    \item Various practitioner implementations: Typically report Sharpe ratios of 0.5-1.0
\end{itemize}

Our higher Sharpe ratio likely reflects: (1) the favorable backtest period, (2) leverage amplification, and (3) the dividend quality screen that may reduce exposure to lower-quality momentum stocks.

\subsection{Implementation Considerations}

Practitioners considering implementation should note:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Capacity}: The strategy trades in liquid large-cap stocks, supporting reasonable capacity. However, position sizes should be calibrated to avoid excessive market impact.

    \item \textbf{Tax Efficiency}: High turnover implies significant short-term capital gains. Tax-advantaged accounts may be preferable.

    \item \textbf{Rebalancing Timing}: Bi-weekly rebalancing on fixed dates may create predictable trading patterns. Randomization or market-condition-based timing may improve execution.

    \item \textbf{Parameter Sensitivity}: The quality threshold ($\kappa = 0.75$), number of holdings ($K = 12$), and rebalancing frequency (14 days) should be stress-tested for robustness.
\end{enumerate}

%==============================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
%==============================================================================

This paper presents a systematic equity strategy combining dual momentum with inverse dividend yield weighting. The strategy selects from a quality-screened universe of 132 dividend-paying stocks, applying absolute momentum as a timing filter and relative momentum for cross-sectional selection. The novel inverse yield weighting scheme uses dividend yield compression as an independent confirmation of momentum strength.

Backtesting with 2$\times$ leverage from March 2020 to November 2025 demonstrates:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Total return of 1,942.77\% (19.4$\times$ initial capital)
    \item Annualized return of 64.56\%
    \item Sharpe ratio of 1.31
    \item Maximum drawdown of $-35.70\%$
    \item Annualized alpha of 31.79\% vs. S\&P 500
\end{itemize}

While these results are impressive, we emphasize that the backtest period represents a particularly favorable environment for momentum strategies. The strategy's true risk-adjusted performance can only be validated through out-of-sample testing across diverse market regimes, including extended bear markets and momentum crash episodes.

Future research directions include:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Extended historical backtesting (pre-2020)
    \item Alternative weighting schemes (risk parity, minimum variance)
    \item Dynamic leverage based on market volatility
    \item International market application
    \item Machine learning enhancements for signal generation
\end{enumerate}

The mathematical framework and systematic implementation presented here provide a foundation for further research and practical application, while the documented limitations serve as important guideposts for realistic expectation setting.

\section{Mathematical Derivations}
\label{app:derivations}

\subsection{Inverse Yield Weight Derivation}

For a portfolio of $K$ stocks, the inverse yield weight for stock $i$ is derived as follows:

Let $Y_i$ denote the dividend yield of stock $i$. The inverse yield score is:
\[
s_i = \frac{1}{Y_i}
\]

The raw weight is normalized to sum to 1:
\[
w_i = \frac{s_i}{\sum_{j=1}^{K} s_j} = \frac{1/Y_i}{\sum_{j=1}^{K} 1/Y_j}
\]

With leverage $\lambda$:
\[
w_i^{\text{lev}} = \lambda \cdot w_i = \frac{\lambda / Y_i}{\sum_{j=1}^{K} 1/Y_j}
\]

\subsection{Portfolio Return Attribution}

The portfolio return at time $t$ can be decomposed as:

\[
r_{p,t} = \underbrace{\sum_{i \in \mathcal{S}} w_i r_{i,t}}_{\text{Gross return}} - \underbrace{\frac{C_t^{\text{slip}}}{E_{t-1}}}_{\text{Slippage drag}} - \underbrace{\frac{C_t^{\text{borrow}}}{E_{t-1}}}_{\text{Borrowing cost}}
\]

where $E_{t-1}$ is the portfolio equity at the previous period.

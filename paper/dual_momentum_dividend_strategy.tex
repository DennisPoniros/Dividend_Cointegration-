\begin{abstract}
This paper presents a systematic equity selection strategy that applies cross-sectional momentum with a momentum-strength filter and inverse dividend yield weighting. We apply the strategy to a universe of 132 dividend-paying U.S. large-cap stocks, employing a per-stock absolute momentum filter and relative momentum for cross-sectional selection. The key innovation lies in weighting selected securities inversely proportional to their trailing dividend yield, exploiting the economic relationship between yield compression and price appreciation. Our backtest spanning March 2020 to November 2025 demonstrates strong performance: with 2$\times$ leverage, total return of 1,942.77\% (geometric), arithmetic mean annualized return of 64.56\%, and Sharpe ratio of 1.31. Importantly, the unleveraged (1$\times$) version achieves nearly identical risk-adjusted performance (Sharpe ratio 1.30), confirming that leverage amplifies returns without improving the strategy's fundamental quality. We provide comprehensive mathematical formalization, detailed cost attribution, and extensive discussion of limitations including favorable period selection, survivorship bias, and execution assumptions. Results should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating pending validation across diverse market regimes.
\end{abstract}

\textbf{Keywords:} Momentum investing, dividend yield, cross-sectional momentum, quantitative strategy, portfolio construction, risk-adjusted returns

\section{Introduction}
%==============================================================================

Momentum strategies have been a cornerstone of quantitative finance since the seminal work of \citet{jegadeesh1993returns}, who documented that stocks with strong past returns tend to continue outperforming over intermediate horizons. This anomaly persists across asset classes, geographies, and time periods, presenting both an empirical puzzle and a profitable trading opportunity.

Parallel to momentum research, dividend-paying stocks have attracted considerable academic and practitioner attention. The dividend yield has been shown to predict both individual stock returns and aggregate market returns, serving as both a valuation metric and a quality signal \citep{fama1988dividend}. Companies with consistent dividend payment histories typically exhibit superior earnings stability, lower leverage, and more disciplined capital allocation.

This paper introduces a quantitative strategy that synthesizes these two research streams through a novel application: using inverse dividend yield as a weighting mechanism within a dividend-paying stock universe. The core economic intuition is that within a universe of dividend-paying stocks, a lower dividend yield---holding dividend policy relatively stable---reflects recent price appreciation, providing an independent signal correlated with momentum strength.

\subsection{Contribution}

Our primary contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Inverse Yield Weighting}: We propose using the reciprocal of dividend yield as portfolio weights within a momentum-selected subset, exploiting the economic relationship between yield compression and price appreciation.

    \item \textbf{Momentum-Strength Filter}: We implement a signal-to-noise filter requiring momentum to exceed a threshold proportion of volatility, reducing exposure to noisy or mean-reverting signals.

    \item \textbf{Comprehensive Backtest with Limitations}: We provide detailed backtest results including cost attribution and benchmark comparison, with explicit acknowledgment of period selection bias and other limitations.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Important Disclaimers}

Before proceeding, we emphasize several critical caveats:

\begin{itemize}
    \item The backtest period (March 2020--November 2025) represents a \textbf{favorable environment} for momentum strategies, beginning near the COVID-19 market bottom.
    \item The stock universe is constructed from \textbf{current} (2025) dividend payers, introducing survivorship bias.
    \item Results should be treated as \textbf{exploratory} and hypothesis-generating, not as evidence of a robust, implementable strategy.
\end{itemize}

\section{Literature Review}
\label{sec:literature}
%==============================================================================

\subsection{Momentum Anomaly}

The momentum effect, first systematically documented by \citet{jegadeesh1993returns}, demonstrates that stocks with high returns over the past 3-12 months continue to outperform over subsequent months. This finding has been replicated extensively and remains one of the most robust anomalies in finance.

\citet{carhart1997persistence} formalized momentum as a risk factor, extending the Fama-French three-factor model. The momentum factor (UMD, or ``Up Minus Down'') captures the return difference between past winners and losers.

\subsection{Dual Momentum and Related Approaches}

\citet{antonacci2014dual} introduced the dual momentum concept, combining relative (cross-sectional) momentum with absolute (time-series) momentum. In Antonacci's canonical implementation, absolute momentum is applied at the \textbf{asset-class level}: when equity momentum is negative, the strategy switches entirely to bonds or cash.

\textbf{Important Distinction}: Our implementation differs from Antonacci's dual momentum in that we apply the absolute momentum filter at the \textbf{individual stock level} rather than at the asset-class level. We do not implement an ``if broad market momentum is negative, move to cash'' overlay. Our strategy is more accurately described as \textbf{cross-sectional momentum with a per-stock momentum-strength filter}, though it shares the conceptual foundation of filtering for positive momentum before ranking.

\subsection{Dividend Yield and Stock Returns}

The dividend yield has a rich history as a return predictor. \citet{fama1988dividend} demonstrated its ability to forecast aggregate market returns, while \citet{campbell1988stock} showed yield predicts returns through both expected return and dividend growth channels.

At the individual stock level, high dividend yield stocks have been associated with the value premium \citep{fama1992cross}. However, the relationship is nuanced: very high yields may signal financial distress (``yield traps''), while moderate yields often indicate quality.

\subsection{Quality Investing}

The quality factor, formalized by \citet{asness2019quality}, encompasses characteristics including profitability, earnings stability, and low leverage. Dividend-paying stocks naturally screen for many quality characteristics, as consistent dividend payment requires stable cash flows.

Our strategy leverages this implicit quality screen by restricting the universe to established dividend payers, then using momentum and yield dynamics for selection and weighting.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}
%==============================================================================

\subsection{Mathematical Framework}

Let $\mathcal{U}$ denote our investment universe of $N$ dividend-paying stocks. For stock $i$ at time $t$, we define:

\begin{itemize}
    \item $P_{i,t}$: Adjusted closing price
    \item $D_{i,t}$: Dividend per share (trailing 12 months)
    \item $r_{i,t}$: Daily simple return
    \item $\sigma_{i,t}$: Annualized volatility
    \item $Y_{i,t}$: Trailing 12-month dividend yield
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Momentum Calculation}

The 12-month momentum for stock $i$ at time $t$ is defined as:

\begin{equation}
\text{MOM}_{i,t} = \frac{P_{i,t}}{P_{i,t-252}} - 1
\label{eq:momentum}
\end{equation}

where 252 represents the number of trading days in one year.

\subsubsection{Volatility Estimation}

We estimate annualized volatility using a 63-day (quarterly) rolling window:

\begin{equation}
\sigma_{i,t} = \sqrt{252} \cdot \sqrt{\frac{1}{62} \sum_{j=1}^{63} \left( r_{i,t-j+1} - \bar{r}_{i,t} \right)^2}
\label{eq:volatility}
\end{equation}

where $\bar{r}_{i,t}$ is the sample mean over the estimation window.

\subsubsection{Momentum-Strength Filter}

To reduce exposure to noisy signals, we require momentum to exceed a threshold proportion of volatility:

\begin{definition}[Momentum-Strength Filter]
Stock $i$ passes the momentum-strength filter at time $t$ if:
\begin{equation}
\text{MOM}_{i,t} > \kappa \cdot \sigma_{i,t}
\label{eq:strength_filter}
\end{equation}
where $\kappa = 0.75$ is the threshold multiplier.
\end{definition}

This condition requires the 12-month return to exceed 0.75 times the recent annualized volatility---a minimum ``Sharpe-like'' threshold for the momentum signal. Note that this filter \textbf{implies} positive momentum ($\text{MOM}_{i,t} > 0$) since $\kappa > 0$ and $\sigma_{i,t} > 0$.

\begin{remark}
We term this a ``momentum-strength filter'' rather than a ``quality filter'' to avoid confusion with the quality factor literature \citep{asness2019quality}, which refers to fundamental characteristics like profitability and stability.
\end{remark}

\subsubsection{Dividend Yield}

The trailing 12-month dividend yield is computed from \textbf{unadjusted} closing prices:

\begin{equation}
Y_{i,t} = \frac{\text{TTM Dividends}_{i,t}}{P_{i,t}^{\text{raw}}}
\label{eq:dividend_yield}
\end{equation}

We impose a floor of 0.5\% to prevent extreme weights:

\begin{equation}
\tilde{Y}_{i,t} = \max(Y_{i,t}, 0.005)
\label{eq:yield_floor}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Selection Algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Cross-Sectional Momentum with Inverse Yield Weighting}
\label{alg:selection}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Universe $\mathcal{U}$, date $t$, parameters $\kappa$, $K$, $\lambda$
\Ensure Selected stocks $\mathcal{S}_t$ with weights $\{w_{i,t}\}$

\State \textbf{Step 1: Momentum-Strength Filter}
\State $\mathcal{Q}_t \gets \{i \in \mathcal{U} : \text{MOM}_{i,t} > \kappa \cdot \sigma_{i,t}\}$
\Comment{Implies MOM $> 0$}

\State \textbf{Step 2: Relative Momentum Ranking}
\State Rank stocks in $\mathcal{Q}_t$ by $\text{MOM}_{i,t}$ in descending order

\State \textbf{Step 3: Top-$K$ Selection}
\State $\mathcal{S}_t \gets$ top $K$ stocks from ranked list

\State \textbf{Step 4: Inverse Yield Weighting (Normalized)}
\For{$i \in \mathcal{S}_t$}
    \State $\omega_i \gets 1 / \tilde{Y}_{i,t}$
\EndFor
\State $w^{(1)}_{i,t} \gets \omega_i / \sum_{j \in \mathcal{S}_t} \omega_j$ \Comment{Normalized weights, sum to 1}

\State \textbf{Step 5: Apply Leverage}
\State $w_{i,t} \gets \lambda \cdot w^{(1)}_{i,t}$ \Comment{Gross exposure weights, sum to $\lambda$}

\State \Return $\mathcal{S}_t$, $\{w_{i,t}\}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Inverse Yield Weighting: Economic Rationale}

The inverse yield weighting scheme rests on the following economic intuition:

\begin{remark}[Yield Compression and Price Appreciation]
Within a universe of dividend-paying stocks, dividend policy tends to be relatively stable in the short term (dividends are ``sticky''). When a stock's price appreciates faster than its dividends grow, the dividend yield compresses. Thus, \textbf{lower yield within the dividend-paying universe is associated with recent price appreciation}, providing a signal correlated with---but distinct from---the momentum signal.
\end{remark}

This is \textbf{not} a formal mathematical theorem; it is an economic intuition that depends on:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Dividend stickiness (dividends adjust slowly relative to prices)
    \item The universe being restricted to established dividend payers
    \item No extreme cases where low yield reflects fundamental deterioration
\end{enumerate}

The inverse yield weight for stock $i$ is:

\begin{equation}
w^{(1)}_{i,t} = \frac{1/\tilde{Y}_{i,t}}{\sum_{j \in \mathcal{S}_t} 1/\tilde{Y}_{j,t}}
\label{eq:inverse_yield_weight}
\end{equation}

With leverage $\lambda$, gross exposure weights become:

\begin{equation}
w_{i,t} = \lambda \cdot w^{(1)}_{i,t}
\label{eq:leveraged_weight}
\end{equation}

For $\lambda = 2.0$, gross weights sum to 2.0, meaning total exposure is 200\% of equity.

\subsection{Leverage Implementation}

We implement leverage by borrowing at a rate proxied by the 10-Year Treasury yield. The margin debt at time $t$ is:

\begin{equation}
M_t = (\lambda - 1) \cdot E_t
\label{eq:margin_debt}
\end{equation}

where $E_t$ is portfolio equity. Daily borrowing cost is:

\begin{equation}
C_t^{\text{borrow}} = M_t \cdot \frac{r_f^{(t)}}{252}
\label{eq:borrowing_cost}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Important caveat}: Using the 10-Year Treasury yield as the borrowing rate likely \textbf{understates} actual margin borrowing costs, which typically exceed short-term rates. This is a simplifying assumption that favors the strategy.

\subsection{Transaction Costs}

We model transaction costs as proportional slippage:

\begin{equation}
C_t^{\text{slip}} = \delta \cdot |V_t^{\text{trade}}|
\label{eq:slippage}
\end{equation}

where $\delta$ represents execution cost per dollar traded.

\subsubsection{Slippage Rate Justification}

Our baseline assumption of $\delta = 0.0002$ (2 basis points) is informed by NASDAQ Economic Research \citep{mackintosh2021spreads}, which documents bid-ask spreads for S\&P 500 constituents:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Top 10 stocks}: $\sim$2.5 bps average spread
    \item \textbf{Top 100 stocks}: $\sim$3.7 bps average spread
    \item \textbf{Full S\&P 500}: $\sim$4.5 bps average spread (plateau)
\end{itemize}

Our universe of 132 large-cap dividend stocks has liquidity characteristics similar to the top-100 S\&P 500 constituents. The 2 bps assumption represents approximately the half-spread for liquid stocks under normal conditions.

\textbf{Caveats}: Actual execution costs may exceed this assumption due to:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Market impact for larger orders
    \item Spread widening during volatility (spreads spiked to $\sim$20 bps during March 2020)
    \item Timing constraints requiring aggressive execution
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Execution Assumptions}

To ensure no look-ahead bias:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Signals are computed using data available at market close on day $t-1$
    \item Trades are executed at the close of day $t$ (next-day execution)
    \item Dividends and ex-dividend dates are properly lagged
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Risk Metrics}

We report the following metrics:

\textbf{Annualized Return (Arithmetic Mean)}:
\begin{equation}
\bar{r}_{\text{ann}} = \bar{r}_{\text{daily}} \times 252
\end{equation}

\textbf{CAGR (Geometric)}:
\begin{equation}
r_{\text{CAGR}} = \left(\frac{E_T}{E_0}\right)^{1/T} - 1
\end{equation}

\textbf{Sharpe Ratio}:
\begin{equation}
\text{SR} = \frac{\bar{r}_{\text{ann}} - r_f}{\sigma_{\text{ann}}}
\label{eq:sharpe}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Maximum Drawdown}:
\begin{equation}
\text{MDD} = \max_{t} \left( \frac{\max_{s \leq t} E_s - E_t}{\max_{s \leq t} E_s} \right)
\label{eq:max_drawdown}
\end{equation}

\section{Data and Universe Construction}
\label{sec:data}
%==============================================================================

\subsection{Stock Universe}

Our investment universe consists of 132 U.S. large-cap stocks satisfying:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Market Capitalization}: Greater than \$5 billion
    \item \textbf{Dividend History}: At least 5 consecutive years of dividend payments
    \item \textbf{Liquidity}: Average daily trading volume exceeding \$10 million
    \item \textbf{Exclusions}: REITs, MLPs, closed-end funds
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Critical limitation}: The universe is constructed from \textbf{current (2025) dividend payers} and applied retrospectively. This introduces \textbf{survivorship bias}: stocks that were dividend payers in 2020 but subsequently cut dividends or delisted are excluded from the backtest. This likely \textbf{overstates} historical performance.

\subsection{Data Sources}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Price Data}: Daily OHLCV adjusted for splits and dividends (for returns)
    \item \textbf{Raw Prices}: Unadjusted closes (for yield calculation)
    \item \textbf{Dividend Data}: Ex-dividend dates and per-share amounts
    \item \textbf{Benchmark}: S\&P 500 (SPY) total return
    \item \textbf{Risk-Free Rate}: 10-Year Treasury (TNX)
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Backtest Period Selection}

The backtest spans \textbf{March 19, 2020 to November 28, 2025} (1,433 trading days, 5.69 years).

\subsubsection{Rationale for Period Selection}

We selected this period for the following reasons:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Recency}: More recent data is generally more relevant for forward-looking inference, as market microstructure, participant behavior, and factor dynamics evolve over time.

    \item \textbf{Data Quality}: Modern dividend and price data is more reliable and consistently recorded.

    \item \textbf{Execution Realism}: Transaction costs, liquidity, and execution quality in the 2020s better reflect current implementability than data from earlier decades.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Acknowledged Bias}

The period beginning March 2020 (near the COVID-19 market bottom) represents one of the \textbf{strongest momentum environments in recent history}:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Extended bull market with limited corrections
    \item Strong sector momentum (technology, healthcare)
    \item Historically low interest rates (favorable for leveraged strategies)
\end{itemize}

The strategy's performance during this period \textbf{cannot} be extrapolated to different regimes, particularly:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Extended bear markets (2008--2009)
    \item Momentum crash episodes (March 2009 reversal)
    \item Prolonged sideways/range-bound markets
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Return Calculation Clarification}

We report two annualized return measures:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Arithmetic Mean} ($\bar{r} \times 252$): The average daily return scaled to annual. This is used in Sharpe ratio calculations and equals 64.56\% (2$\times$) and 32.88\% (1$\times$).

\end{enumerate}

The difference arises because arithmetic mean exceeds geometric mean for volatile return series (Jensen's inequality).

\section{Sources of Alpha}
\label{sec:alpha}
%==============================================================================

We hypothesize several potential sources of the strategy's outperformance:

\subsection{Momentum Premium}

The momentum anomaly is well-documented and persistent \citep{jegadeesh1993returns}. By selecting stocks with strong 12-month returns, we capture the cross-sectional momentum premium.

\subsection{Implicit Quality Screen}

The dividend-paying universe excludes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Unprofitable companies (cannot sustain dividends)
    \item Highly leveraged firms (prioritize debt service over dividends)
    \item Young/speculative companies (no dividend history)
\end{itemize}

This provides an implicit quality tilt without explicit quality factor construction.

\subsection{Momentum-Strength Filter}

Requiring $\text{MOM} > 0.75 \times \sigma$ filters out:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Low-conviction momentum signals dominated by noise
    \item Mean-reverting stocks that happen to have positive 12-month returns
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Inverse Yield Weighting}

The inverse yield weighting potentially:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Amplifies exposure to stocks with strongest recent appreciation
    \item Introduces a growth/momentum tilt within the dividend universe
    \item Avoids ``yield traps'' (distressed stocks with unsustainably high yields)
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Caveat}: These are \textit{hypotheses} about alpha sources, not proven mechanisms. Factor attribution against standard models (Fama-French, momentum, quality) would be needed to isolate true alpha from factor exposures.

\subsection{Period-Specific Factors}

The 2020--2025 period exhibited:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Strong performance of growth/low-yield stocks
    \item Sustained momentum in technology and healthcare
    \item Low interest rates (reducing borrowing costs for leverage)
\end{itemize}

Much of the apparent alpha may be period-specific rather than a durable edge.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
%==============================================================================

\subsection{Strengths}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Consistent Risk-Adjusted Performance}: The Sharpe ratio of $\sim$1.3 is strong, and importantly, nearly identical at 1$\times$ and 2$\times$ leverage.

    \item \textbf{Systematic Implementation}: All rules are mathematically specified and mechanically implementable.

    \item \textbf{Reasonable Turnover Costs}: At baseline 2 bps slippage, costs consume 9.4\% of gross P\&L.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Limitations}

\subsubsection{Favorable Backtest Period}

The March 2020 start date is near the COVID-19 market bottom---one of the best entry points in market history for momentum strategies. Performance during:
\begin{itemize}
    \item 2008--2009 financial crisis
    \item 2000--2002 dot-com bust
    \item Sideways/choppy markets
\end{itemize}
remains untested and likely worse.

\subsubsection{Survivorship Bias}

The universe is constructed from 2025 dividend payers applied retrospectively. Stocks that cut dividends or delisted are excluded, biasing results upward.

\subsubsection{Execution Assumptions}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Slippage}: 2 bps may be optimistic for larger portfolios or volatile periods
    \item \textbf{Borrowing Rate}: 10-Year Treasury understates actual margin costs
    \item \textbf{Market Impact}: Not modeled; relevant for larger implementations
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Parameter Sensitivity}

Key parameters ($\kappa = 0.75$, $K = 12$, 14-day rebalance) were not robustness-tested across wide ranges. Results may be sensitive to these choices.

\subsubsection{No Out-of-Sample Validation}

All results are in-sample. True strategy quality requires:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Walk-forward testing
    \item Paper trading
    \item Live implementation
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Comparison to Literature}

The Sharpe ratio of 1.3 compares favorably to academic momentum studies (typically 0.4--0.6) and practitioner implementations (0.5--1.0). However:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Our backtest is \textbf{shorter} (5.7 years vs. decades)
    \item Our period is \textbf{more favorable} (post-COVID bull market)
    \item Our universe is \textbf{more concentrated} (132 stocks vs. broad market)
    \item Survivorship bias inflates our results
\end{itemize}

Direct comparison is inappropriate; our results are exploratory.

%==============================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
%==============================================================================

This paper presents a cross-sectional momentum strategy with inverse dividend yield weighting, applied to a universe of 132 large-cap U.S. dividend payers. Key findings:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Strong in-sample performance}: 1,942.77\% total return (2$\times$ leverage), Sharpe ratio 1.31
    \item \textbf{Leverage-neutral risk-adjustment}: Sharpe ratio nearly identical at 1$\times$ (1.30) and 2$\times$ (1.31) leverage
    \item \textbf{Significant caveats}: Favorable period, survivorship bias, optimistic cost assumptions
\end{itemize}

We emphasize that these results are \textbf{exploratory and hypothesis-generating}. The strategy requires:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Extended historical testing (pre-2020)
    \item Out-of-sample validation
    \item Robustness testing across parameters
    \item Factor attribution to isolate true alpha
    \item Live paper trading before implementation
\end{enumerate}

The mathematical framework and systematic implementation provide a foundation for further research, while the documented limitations serve as guideposts for realistic expectation setting.
